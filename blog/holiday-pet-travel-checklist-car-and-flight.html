Traveling With a Pet for the Holidays: Car and Flight Checklist

Guide14 min read

By Shaheen Arif, Founder and Editor · Editorially reviewed by Talha N. Ch for clarity, sourcing and product-claim accuracy. Not reviewed by a veterinarian · About us · Updated November 19, 2026

Holiday trips are hard on pets in ways an ordinary weekend is not. Roads and airports are busier, the weather can turn quickly, and the house you arrive at is full of new people, new smells and food within reach. A little planning removes most of that stress.

This guide covers traveling with a pet for the holidays step by step, for US travelers: the vet visit and paperwork, a car checklist, a flight checklist, a packing list and settling in at the other end. It flags where the rules depend on your airline, destination or pet's health, so you know what to confirm.

Quick answer

To travel with a dog or cat over the holidays, you need a vet check and any health certificate your airline or destination asks for, a secure carrier or crash-tested restraint, ID tags and an up-to-date microchip, enough food and medication for the whole trip plus extra, water, cleanup supplies and a calm, pet-safe space waiting at your destination.

  • Confirm your airline's current pet policy and your destination's entry rules before you book.
  • Book a vet visit two to four weeks ahead so there is time to fix paperwork problems.
  • Practice with the carrier, harness or car seat before the trip, not on travel day.
  • Pack familiar food, bedding and a recent photo of your pet.
  • Plan for holiday hazards at the destination: food scraps, decorations and open doors.

How to use this checklist

Start with the master table below, then read the section for the way you are traveling and work through the packing list a few days before you leave. If your pet is very young or old, has a health condition, or is a snub-nosed breed such as a pug or Persian cat, talk to your vet before you commit to a plan; sometimes a trusted sitter at home is the kinder choice.

Tip: Save this page to your phone and tick items off as you go. Add your vet's number, your pet's microchip number and the nearest 24-hour emergency vet at your destination to the top of your copy.

Before you go: vet visit, paperwork and ID

Book the vet visit early

A pre-trip appointment two to four weeks before you leave gives you time to update vaccines, refill prescriptions and sort out any certificate your trip needs. Tell your vet how you are traveling, for how long and where. Ask whether your pet is fit for the journey, what to do about motion sickness, and how to handle feeding on travel day.

Health certificates and destination rules

Many airlines ask for a health certificate, often called a certificate of veterinary inspection, issued within a set window before departure. Some states have their own entry requirements, and Hawaii in particular has strict rules for arriving pets. Requirements differ by airline, destination, species and age, and they change, so ask your airline and your vet what applies to your exact itinerary.

Leaving the country takes far more lead time. Destination countries can require specific vaccines, tests and endorsed paperwork on a strict timeline, and dogs coming into or back into the United States must meet CDC requirements. Start months ahead. If you are flying abroad, our pet travel help service can coordinate the paperwork with you, and the USDA APHIS and CDC pages in the sources below are the official references.

Ask about anxiety, not sedation

Sedating pets for travel, especially for flights, is generally discouraged. Sedatives can affect breathing, balance and temperature control, and a sedated pet in a carrier or cargo hold cannot be watched closely. Never give a pet any medication, including a human product or a leftover prescription, without your vet's direct guidance. If your pet gets anxious or carsick, your vet can suggest options suited to your pet's age and health.

Update ID and microchip details

Pets slip out of doors and carriers more often during busy holidays. Put an ID tag with your cell number on the collar or harness, check that the microchip registry lists your current phone and email, and keep a recent photo of your pet on your phone. A tracker can add reassurance; Traverseon lists a Smart Pet Tracker it describes as IP65 waterproof and compatible with iOS and Android apps. A tracker supplements a tag and microchip; it does not replace them.

When to talk to a veterinarian: Speak with your vet before any trip if your pet is pregnant, very young, elderly, recovering from illness or surgery, has heart or breathing problems, or is a short-nosed (brachycephalic) breed. Seek prompt care during a trip if your pet has trouble breathing, collapses, vomits repeatedly, shows signs of heatstroke or hypothermia, or may have eaten something toxic.

How do I keep my pet safe on a holiday road trip?

Restrain your pet every time the car moves

A loose pet can distract the driver and be thrown forward in a sudden stop. Cats and small dogs usually travel best in a carrier secured so it cannot slide or tip, on the back seat or the floor. Larger dogs can ride in a crash-tested harness attached to the seat belt system, a secured crate, or the cargo area behind a barrier. Keep pets out of the front seat, where an airbag can injure them.

Traverseon lists a 3-in-1 Pet Car Seat & Carrier, described as a washable travel bed for pets up to 28 lbs, and an Adjustable Dog Car Barrier, a foldable metal divider for the cargo area. Check fit against your vehicle and your pet's weight, and look for any crash-test claims on the product page rather than assuming them.

Get your pet used to the car first

If your pet only rides in the car to visit the vet, a long drive can be a shock. In the weeks before, take short drives that end somewhere pleasant, and leave the carrier out at home with a familiar blanket inside.

Plan stops, feeding and water

Many owners plan a stop every couple of hours so dogs can drink, stretch and relieve themselves; keep the leash on at every stop, because rest areas are busy. Cats generally stay in the carrier; on a long drive, offer water and a small litter tray inside the parked, locked car. A light meal a few hours before leaving, rather than right before, can help with motion sickness. A leak-proof bottle makes roadside water easy; Traverseon lists a Portable Collapsible Pet Water Bottle with a one-hand design and a collapsible drinking cup.

Never leave a pet alone in a parked car

Car interiors heat up fast in sun, even on mild days, and cool down fast in winter. Holiday stops at crowded stores often take longer than planned. Use drive-throughs, take turns going inside, or choose pet-friendly stops. If you stay overnight, confirm the hotel's pet rules before you set off.

Pack a winter-weather car kit

Holiday driving often means snow, ice and closures. Add an extra day of food and water, a warm blanket, a towel for wet paws and a spare leash to your winter kit. Wipe paws after walks on treated sidewalks, since de-icing salts can irritate pads, and watch for spilled antifreeze at gas stations: ethylene glycol antifreeze is highly toxic to dogs and cats.

What should I know before flying with a pet for the holidays?

Read your airline's current pet policy first

Airline pet rules differ a lot and change, so read the current policy for your airline and route before you buy a ticket. Look for whether pets can fly in the cabin or the hold, carrier size limits, breed and age restrictions, health certificate requirements, fees, and any seasonal limits on pets in the hold during very hot or cold weather. Some airlines restrict snub-nosed breeds. Call the airline if anything is unclear.

Book early and add your pet to the reservation

Airlines typically limit how many pets can travel in the cabin on each flight, and holiday flights fill early. Add your pet to the reservation as soon as your plans are firm and get confirmation. Choose nonstop flights where you can and avoid tight connections.

Choose and measure the carrier

For in-cabin travel, your pet usually stays in a carrier under the seat in front of you for the whole flight. Under-seat space varies by aircraft, so check your airline's published dimensions. Your pet should be able to stand, turn around and lie down inside. Two carriers already featured on this site are the Traverseon Top-Opening Foldable Pet Carrier, a ventilated carrier the retailer markets for air and car travel, and the 3-Way Anti-Escape Pet Carrier Backpack, listed for cats and small dogs. Whatever a product page says about airline approval, your own airline's limits are what count at the gate.

Get through airport security calmly

At TSA screening you will usually be asked to take your pet out of the carrier and carry it through the walk-through detector while the empty carrier is X-rayed. Put a snug harness and leash on your pet before you reach the airport, especially a cat. If you are worried, ask officers whether a private screening room is available, and arrive early.

Food, water and comfort on the flight

Ask your vet how much to feed before a flight. Line the carrier with an absorbent pad and a familiar blanket, and pack a spare pad in a zip bag. Bring an empty bottle or collapsible bowl through security and fill it afterward. Keep medications, some food and cleanup supplies in your carry-on, not checked luggage. If you travel with an emotional support or service animal, read your airline's current policy and complete any forms well before travel day.

Holiday pet travel packing list

Pack your pet's bag a few days early. Traverseon lists a 13-Piece Pet Travel Organizer Bag if you want everything in one place, but any sturdy bag works.

Documents

  • Vaccination records and any health certificate, on paper and on your phone
  • Microchip number, your vet's number and a 24-hour emergency vet near your destination
  • Airline confirmation showing your pet on the booking, if flying
  • A recent photo of your pet

Food, water and medication

  • Your pet's usual food for the whole trip plus a few extra days, in case of delays
  • Treats, a collapsible bowl and a travel water bottle
  • All medications and supplements, with dosing instructions, kept within reach

Comfort and containment

  • Carrier, crate, car seat or crash-tested harness
  • Collar with ID tag, a well-fitted harness and a spare leash
  • Familiar bedding and one or two favorite toys

Cleanup and hygiene

  • Waste bags, paper towels and pet-safe wipes
  • Absorbent pads; MeoWant lists Pet Training Pads with six layers and a leak-proof backing, useful for lining a carrier or protecting a host's floor
  • For cats: a small foldable or disposable litter tray, their usual litter and a scoop
  • Enzyme cleaner for accidents and a towel for wet paws

At the destination: settling in and avoiding holiday hazards

Set up a quiet safe room

Before your pet explores, set up one quiet room with their bed, water, food and, for cats, a litter tray. Let your pet settle there first, and return them to it during arrivals, meals and gift opening. Ask hosts and guests to keep that door closed and to watch outside doors; a frightened pet can bolt in an unfamiliar neighborhood.

Keep the routine as normal as you can

Feed at the usual times with the usual food, walk dogs on their normal schedule and keep bedtime familiar. Ask guests clearly not to share table scraps, because well-meaning relatives often do.

Watch for holiday hazards

  • Food: chocolate, xylitol-sweetened foods, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, alcohol and cooked bones can all harm pets. Keep plates and trash out of reach.
  • Plants: lilies are extremely dangerous to cats, even in small amounts. Ask hosts to keep them out of the house during your stay.
  • Decorations: tinsel, ribbon and string can cause serious problems if a cat swallows them. Secure the tree, hide cords and keep candles out of reach.
  • Other animals: introduce your pet to resident pets slowly and supervise until everyone is relaxed.
  • Cold: limit time outdoors in freezing weather, especially for small, short-coated or older pets.

If you think your pet has eaten something toxic, call your vet or an emergency vet right away. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is also available at (888) 426-4435; a consultation fee may apply.

Be a considerate guest

Clean up promptly, bring your own supplies, and keep litter odor under control in a small guest room by scooping at least daily. Common holiday pet travel mistakes

  • Booking before checking the pet policy: there may be no space for your pet on that flight; confirm first.
  • Buying a carrier on the way out the door: an unfamiliar carrier adds stress; buy early and practice.
  • Assuming last year's rules still apply: airline and entry requirements change; check every trip.
  • Sedating without vet advice: this can be dangerous; ask your vet about safer options.
  • Letting a pet ride loose in the car: use a carrier, harness, car seat or barrier.
  • Leaving food and decorations within reach: pet-proof the safe room before you unpack.

Limitations and checks

This guide is general information for US travelers, not veterinary, legal or airline advice. Airline policies, fees, carrier dimensions and health certificate rules vary by airline, route and aircraft and change over time, so confirm them with your airline before booking and again shortly before you travel. Entry rules are set by government agencies. Product details come from retailer listings; we have not tested these products, so confirm fit and specifications on the product page.

When to talk to a veterinarian: Your vet is the right person to decide whether your pet is fit to travel, what paperwork the trip needs and how to manage motion sickness or anxiety. Do not give any sedative or medication for travel unless your vet has recommended it for your pet.

Bottom line

Traveling with a pet for the holidays goes well when the hard parts happen early: a vet visit with time to spare, a confirmed booking for your pet if you fly, a carrier or restraint your pet already knows, and a packed bag with food, medication and records. Keep your pet secured on the way, never leave them in a parked car, and set up a quiet, pet-proofed room when you arrive. Check your airline's current pet policy and talk to your vet; those two conversations settle most of what matters.

Frequently asked questions

What do I need to travel with a dog or cat over the holidays?

You need a vet check, any required health certificate, a secure carrier or crash-tested restraint, an ID tag and up-to-date microchip, your pet's usual food and medication plus extra, water, cleanup supplies and copies of records. Confirm your airline's current pet policy before booking.

Do I need a health certificate to travel with my pet?

It depends on how and where you travel. Many airlines ask for a health certificate issued within a set window before departure, and some states and all international destinations have entry rules. Ask your airline and your vet what applies to your exact trip, because requirements vary and change.

Should I sedate my pet for a holiday flight?

Sedation for travel is generally discouraged because it can affect breathing, balance and temperature control. Never give a pet any medication for travel unless your vet has recommended it, and ask your vet about safer ways to manage anxiety.

How should a dog or cat ride in the car?

Cats and small dogs usually travel best in a carrier secured so it cannot slide or tip, on the back seat or the floor. Larger dogs can use a crash-tested harness attached to the seat belt system, a secured crate or a cargo-area barrier. Keep pets out of the front seat and never let them ride loose.

How often should I stop on a road trip with a dog?

Many owners plan a stop every couple of hours so a dog can drink, stretch and relieve itself on a leash. Adjust for your dog's age, health and bladder, keep the leash on at every stop, and never leave your dog alone in a parked car.

Can my pet fly in the cabin with me?

Many airlines allow small cats and dogs in the cabin in a carrier that fits under the seat in front of you, but rules, fees, size limits and the number of pets allowed per flight vary by airline. Read your airline's current pet policy and add your pet to the booking early, especially for holiday flights.

How do I keep my pet safe at a relative's house during the holidays?

Set up a quiet safe room with bedding, water, food and a litter tray for cats. Keep the usual routine and food, ask guests not to share scraps, keep chocolate, xylitol, grapes, onions, cooked bones, lilies and tinsel out of reach, and watch outside doors.
